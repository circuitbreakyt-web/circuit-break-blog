    AI Drug Discovery's Dirty Secret: Most Molecules Fail

    AI promises to revolutionize drug discovery, but the bottleneck was never finding molecules. The real problem is far harder to automate.
    April 11, 2026 · 5 min · Circuit Break

    In March 2026, Eli Lilly wrote a check for $115 million — with up to $2.75 billion more on the table — to license a set of preclinical molecules that no human chemist designed. The molecules came from Insilico Medicine’s Pharma.AI suite, an AI platform that identifies drug targets, generates novel molecules, and helps plan clinical trials. It was one of the largest AI-drug-discovery deals in history. And yet, as of late 2025, not a single AI-discovered drug has earned FDA approval.

    That tension — massive investment crashing against a stubborn approval gap — is the real story of AI in drug discovery right now. The technology is genuinely compressing timelines and cutting costs in early-stage research. But it hasn’t solved the hardest problem in pharma: getting drugs through clinical trials, where 90% of candidates still fail.

    Faster to the Starting Line, Not to the Finish#

    The speed gains in preclinical development are real and dramatic. Traditional drug discovery takes 3-4 years just to move from target identification to a candidate ready for human trials, at an average total development cost of $2.6 billion per approved drug. AI is compressing that preclinical window to 13-18 months — a 30-40% reduction.

    Insilico Medicine is the clearest proof point. The company advanced an AI-discovered drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) — rentosertib — to Phase II clinical trials in under 30 months, compared to the 6-8 years that’s typical. In 2025, the Phase IIa results were published in Nature Medicine, making it the first peer-reviewed efficacy data for an AI-designed small molecule. Insilico also reports a 70% reduction in the number of compounds it needs to synthesize before finding a viable candidate, translating to roughly 40% cost savings in preclinical work.

    Novartis is taking a different approach to the same problem. In late 2025, the company’s researchers used generative AI to design 15 million potential compounds targeting molecular glue degraders for Huntington’s disease — a scale of chemical exploration that would be unthinkable with traditional methods.

    These aren’t isolated experiments. According to BCG data, more than 75 AI-first biotech drugs and vaccines had entered clinical trials as of January 2024. The pipeline is growing fast.

    But here’s the counterintuitive part: AI is getting molecules into clinical trials faster without meaningfully improving the odds once they get there. The 90% clinical failure rate — driven by human biology’s complexity, unforeseen toxicity, and the gap between animal models and human response — remains stubbornly intact. AI, so far, is a speed multiplier for the easy part.

    The Infrastructure Play Happening Underneath#

    While the headlines focus on AI-designed molecules, a quieter but potentially more consequential shift is happening in data infrastructure.

    Drug discovery has always been bottlenecked by fragmented data — genomics data sitting in one system, clinical imaging in another, real-world patient outcomes in a third. The companies building connective tissue between these data silos may end up mattering as much as the ones designing molecules.

    Databricks launched AiChemy, a multi-agent AI system that connects enterprise and public datasets for target identification and compound evaluation. In July 2025, Databricks partnered with TileDB to integrate multimodal data — genomics, imaging, and more — into a unified platform for drug research. A month earlier, it teamed with Atropos Health to pull real-world clinical data into its pipeline for faster evidence generation.

    Bayer AG, meanwhile, is applying AI across the entire development cycle. At a September 2025 keynote at the AI Summit in New York, Bayer executives outlined how AI is being deployed not just in discovery but in:

    • Clinical imaging analysis — spotting patterns in radiology and pathology data
    • Patient matching for trials — cutting recruitment timelines by 30-50%
    • Precision medicine — tailoring treatments based on individual patient data

    That patient-matching number matters. Slow enrollment is one of the biggest reasons clinical trials take years and cost billions. If AI can reliably cut recruitment time in half, the downstream effects on cost and speed could be enormous — even if the biological failure rate stays the same.

    What Actually Needs to Happen Next#

    The honest assessment is this: AI has proven it can find promising drug candidates faster and cheaper. It has not yet proven it can find better ones. The gap between 75+ AI-associated clinical trials and zero FDA approvals isn’t just a timing issue. It’s a signal that the hardest problems in drug development — predicting human toxicity, navigating immune responses, understanding long-term effects — remain beyond current AI capabilities.

    The next few years will be decisive. Insilico’s rentosertib and a handful of other AI-originated compounds are now deep enough in clinical trials that we’ll start getting real answers about whether AI-designed drugs can clear the approval bar, not just the preclinical one.

    For professionals watching this space — whether you’re in biotech, healthcare, or data science — the practical takeaway is that AI drug discovery is not hype, but it is incomplete. The tools are real: Pharma.AI for molecular design, Databricks AiChemy for data integration, and an expanding ecosystem around them. The results, at the stage that actually matters to patients, remain unproven. If you want to build genuine intuition for how the underlying neural network architectures work — the kind of understanding that separates informed skepticism from blind faith — Grokking Deep Learning by Andrew Trask is one of the best places to start.

    The billions flowing into this field are a bet that AI will eventually crack clinical trial success rates, not just preclinical timelines. That bet hasn’t paid off yet. But the next 24 months will tell us whether it ever will.
